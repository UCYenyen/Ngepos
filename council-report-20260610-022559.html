Council Verdict — Ngepos

Is a solo-built POS SaaS sellable in Indonesia, against ESB & funded incumbents? 5 advisors · peer-reviewed

The question: Ngepos is a solo-built, multi-tenant SaaS POS for Indonesian F&B/retail SMEs (MVP; Next.js + self-hosted Supabase; tiers Starter/Pro/Enterprise). Honest value prop? Sellable as commercial SaaS? GTM/pricing/distribution? Can a solo entrant beat funded incumbents (ESB, Moka/GoTo, Majoo, Qasir-free) — or should it pivot? Stakes: invest time commercializing, pivot, or treat as a portfolio project.

Where the advisors landed

Don't sell as SaaS
Contrarian — one VPS, no offline = can't be a primary register; you'd charge for the loss-leader. First Principles — POS is a wedge; software is priced at zero. Wrong question. Outsider — invisible (no feet), "built by one person" = fear it vanishes.
Validate first
Executor — stop polishing; get ONE merchant to run a real shift this week; charge coffee-money. Peer review: all 5 picked this as the strongest move.
Go for it
Expansionist — payment-neutral, multi-business, white-label reseller, WhatsApp-native = lanes incumbents won't copy. Peer review: 4/5 flagged this as the biggest blind spot — unvalidated demand.

Chairman's Verdict

Where the council agrees

  • Nobody profits from POS software in Indonesia. Incumbents free/subsidize the app and monetize the payment rail, hardware margin, and lending on transaction data. Charging SaaS for the loss-leader — without payments/hardware/field-sales — is structurally hard.
  • Head-on, a solo founder can't beat ESB/Moka/Qasir on their terms (field sales, in-person install, support). "No mouth, no feet."
  • It's a genuinely strong portfolio / proof-of-competence asset regardless of commercial outcome.
  • The real near-term rival is pen, paper, calculator, or free Qasir — not ESB.
  • The binding unknown is willingness-to-pay, and it's cheap to test now.

Where the council clashes

Commercialize vs. don't. The Expansionist says GO — "payment-neutral, no-lock-in" is a lane incumbents structurally avoid. Contrarian/First Principles say that neutrality is exactly the weakness: it's the builder's preference, not what a warung owner pays for. Peer review sided against the Expansionist — the lanes are assertions with zero demand evidence.

Is the fragile infra a dealbreaker? The Contrarian says one VPS + no offline disqualifies it as a primary register. A reviewer pushed back: that's solvable/deferrable engineering (offline-first PWA, managed hosting); the binding constraint is demand, not uptime. Both true — not a reason to quit, but a reason you can't yet be someone's only cash register.

Blind spots the peer review caught

  • The biggest miss by ALL five: nobody asked what YOU want — income now, learning, a résumé/job signal, or runway. That goal should drive the decision more than any competitor analysis. "Portfolio" is a deliberate high-ROI choice, not a consolation prize.
  • The migration moment — re-keying products/history + retraining staff is the highest-friction barrier, unpriced by everyone. Even a free, better POS loses here.
  • Data trust & continuity — solo dev as system-of-record for money/tax data, one VPS, no backups, no legal entity = liability + "what if he quits?"
  • Regulation — "own payments later" may be legally capped; QRIS acquiring needs licensing.
  • Non-SaaS monetization of the same code — one-time license, self-host for one chain, build-for-hire.

The recommendation

Don't launch it as a horizontal commercial SaaS vs. ESB — that's a losing battle on their turf. But don't shelve it either.

1. Decide your own goal first. If it's "income from this product," the path is narrow and services-heavy. If it's "portfolio / land freelance + job offers / learn" — you've already won: ship it, get 3–5 real warungs live, use it as undeniable proof. For a solo freelancer this is the highest-ROI, most realistic outcome.

2. Regardless of goal, run the one-week validation before building more. Niche down to ONE vertical you can reach in person (cafes/cloud kitchens in your city, or multi-outlet owners who value the portfolio view). White-glove a handful by hand. Coffee-money + unprompted reuse = a real (small) services+software micro-business, NOT venture SaaS. If not, you learned cheaply and the portfolio value stays.

Positioning if you proceed: not "a cheaper POS" but what incumbents won't do — payment-neutral, all-your-businesses-in-one-account, daily sales + restock approval over WhatsApp. Sell the outcome ("lihat omzet semua tokomu tiap hari di WhatsApp"), not the tech.

⭐ The one thing to do first

This week, get ONE real merchant you can reach in person (a friend/family warung or cafe on Qasir-free or paper) to run a full day of real sales on Ngepos — you do the setup + data entry yourself — then ask the next one for Rp 50–99k/month via bank transfer. Their yes/no is worth more than any feature, tier, or strategy.

Full advisor responses

Executor "Stop polishing — one merchant, one shift, this week." ★ strongest (5/5)
Stop polishing the product. You have enough. This week is about one thing: getting a real merchant to run a real shift on Ngepos. Mon–Tue: Pick ONE merchant type and ONE neighbor. Not "F&B and retail SMEs in Indonesia." A single small cafe or warung near you — ideally someone you already know. F&B, single outlet, currently on Qasir-free or a paper notebook. Ignore chains (ESB owns them), ignore anyone who needs hardware or payment settlement — you can't do field sales or money movement solo. Wed: Go there in person. Don't email, don't build a landing page, don't run ads. Sit with the owner during a slow hour: "Show me how you record sales today." Watch them. If they're happy with Qasir-free, leave — that's your answer, free. If they complain about reports, multi-outlet, or staff-theft visibility — that's your wedge. Thu: Set them up live, free, for 30 days. You punch in their products yourself. White-glove. Get one real transaction while standing there. Fri: Charge the next one. If pilot #1 logs sales for 3 days unprompted, you have something. Price Rp 50–99k/month, manual bank transfer, no gateway. If they won't pay a coffee's worth, the market's answer is no. Ignore this week: pricing tiers, Enterprise, automated WhatsApp reports, competitor analysis. One merchant, one shift, one yes/no.
Contrarian "ESB loses to nobody — and your POS dies when the internet dies." ⚑ flagged on infra
ESB doesn't lose to a better-built web app. It loses to nobody, because it shows up in person, installs the hardware, trains the cashier, and answers the phone when the printer jams at 9pm on a Saturday. You are a solo dev with a self-hosted Supabase on one VPS. When that VPS goes down — and it will — a warung's cashier cannot ring up customers. No POS buyer tolerates that. No failover, no on-call, no SLA, no offline mode. A real POS works when the internet dies; yours dies with it. That alone disqualifies it as a primary register for any serious merchant. QRIS-by-image-upload is not a payment feature, it's a screenshot. The incumbents make money on payment acquiring and hardware margins, not SaaS fees — that's why Qasir gives software away free. You're trying to charge SaaS fees in a market where the software is the loss-leader. What's your wedge? Distribution: incumbents have field sales armies in every kota. How does a solo dev acquire, onboard, and support merchants who don't read English, don't trust web apps, and call you when anything breaks? Support alone will consume your entire week at 20 customers. Brutal answer: not sellable as commercial SaaS solo. Treat it as a portfolio piece — a strong one. If you must commercialize, don't compete; pick a narrow niche the giants ignore (a single multi-outlet franchise, or cloud kitchens) and white-glove ten of them by hand.
First Principles "You're asking the wrong, sunk-cost question."
The founder isn't really asking "can my POS compete with ESB?" The real question underneath is: "I've built something competent — how do I get a return on the time I've sunk, and the bigger time I'm about to sink?" That's sunk-cost-driven, and it's the wrong framing. A POS is not a product, it's a wedge. Nobody makes money selling the software — incumbents survive on payment processing take rates, hardware margin, and lending on transaction data. They give the software away or near-free. So "build a POS SaaS and charge tiers" is structurally a losing game: you're charging for the one thing the market has priced at zero, while lacking what it actually monetizes (payments rails, capital, field sales). The right question is: "What do I actually have, and who is so underserved that even a solo dev beats their status quo?" For most micro-SMEs the status-quo competitor isn't ESB — it's pen, paper, and a calculator. Qasir is free and still hasn't swallowed everyone. Reframe to one of three real questions: (1) Learning/portfolio asset? Ship it, get 5 real warungs, let it be proof of competence — high ROI, low delusion. (2) A narrow niche incumbents ignore? Then it's a services-and-software micro-business, not a SaaS. (3) Do you have a payments/distribution partner? If no — you cannot win the SaaS framing. Pick the question. Don't answer the one you brought.
Outsider "What does this do that a notebook doesn't, in one sentence?"
With zero context: (1) What is it? A website where a shop punches in sales instead of using a calculator. Fine. But "multi-tenant SaaS POS gated by subscription plan" means nothing to a warung owner. To them: "a screen that adds up what customers buy and tells me what sold." If you can't say it in one breath, they tune out. (2) Would they pay? The competitors give away hardware and handle the money. Yours is a monthly bill that does LESS and asks for MORE. A warung owner thinks: "Why pay every month forever for something a notebook and calculator already do for free?" The fear isn't the price, it's the recurring-charge-on-a-thing-I-don't-fully-trust. (3) How do they find it? This is the scariest one. Competitors have humans walking into shops. You have a URL. Indonesian small shop owners don't Google "POS software" — they hear about things from other owners, family, the guy who sold them the fridge. You have no mouth and no feet. Right now this is invisible. (4) Why pick you? I genuinely can't answer this from what you gave me — and that's the problem. "Built by one person" is not a reason to buy; it's a reason to worry it disappears. The brutal question: what does this do that a notebook doesn't, that a free competitor doesn't, in one sentence a 50-year-old shop owner believes? Until you can say that, nothing else matters.
Expansionist "Incumbents are chained to the payment rail — that's your lane." ⚑ biggest blind spot (4/5)
Indonesia's incumbents are all chasing the same prize: own the payment rail, clip the transaction. That's where the money is, so that's where 100% of their roadmap goes — and it chains them. Moka/GoBiz must push GoTo's wallet. ESB must sell hardware and field-deploy. They structurally CANNOT serve the merchant who wants neutral software and zero lock-in — because neutral software kills their take-rate. That's your asymmetric lane. Three upsides nobody's pricing in: 1. Payment-neutral, multi-business-by-default. You already support multiple businesses per owner. Indonesian SME owners are serial — a warung, then a coffee cart, then a cloud kitchen. Incumbents bill per-outlet and assume one entity. A flat "manage your whole portfolio" account is a wedge they won't copy because it dilutes per-terminal revenue. 2. The reseller/agency channel. A solo dev can't out-field-sales ESB — so don't. White-label the whole thing to local IT shops, accountants, and koperasi who already own SME relationships in tier-2/3 cities incumbents ignore. You become infrastructure; they do distribution. Funded companies avoid this because it cannibalizes direct sales. 3. WhatsApp-native reporting is a sleeper. You already ship it. Lean in: owner gets daily sales on WA, approves restock from WA. That's the actual Indonesian UX, and a moat of a thousand tiny integrations incumbents won't bother with. Commercialize it. The wedge is real, the build cost is your time, and the downside is a portfolio piece either way. Go.
